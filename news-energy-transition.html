The energy transition does not move forward on announcements alone

Targets can point the way; turning them into results requires technologies integrated into systems that can operate, maintain and demonstrate their performance.

Technology · Infrastructure · Capabilities · Evidence

A technology does not work in isolation. Its performance depends on the technical, economic, institutional and territorial conditions that make it possible to deploy, maintain and assess its results.

A technology may be promising and still fail to produce the expected results. This can happen when:

  • Its performance is not assessed under the territory's real operating conditions.
  • The infrastructure required for operation, maintenance or energy supply is not available.
  • Investment, operating, maintenance and replacement costs are not examined across the full life cycle.
  • System reliability, efficiency, safety and availability are not considered.
  • Baseline data are incomplete, inconsistent or difficult to compare.
  • Local technical capabilities to operate, diagnose, repair and improve the solution do not exist.
  • The technology is not properly integrated with existing equipment, processes and networks.
  • No monitoring mechanisms are in place to measure results and correct deviations.

More than deploying technology

The energy transition requires technologies, but it also requires methods, procedures, planning, budgets, consistent information, maintenance, coordination and monitoring.

Moving from an energy or climate target to an operational solution means translating broad objectives into concrete technical requirements. It also means comparing alternatives according to their performance, costs and implementation conditions; designing projects for the territory; and applying lessons from earlier processes.

The challenge does not end with the selection or procurement of a technology. It continues through integration, operation, maintenance and evaluation.

Questions often left in the background

Before implementing a solution, several basic questions should be answered:

  • Will it work under the territory's real conditions?
  • Is the infrastructure required to put it into operation available?
  • How will it be integrated with existing systems and processes?
  • Who will be able to operate, diagnose and maintain it?
  • What will it cost over its full useful life?
  • What will happen if it fails or performs below expectations?
  • How will its results be measured?
  • What mechanisms will make it possible to correct deviations?

These questions are not intended to hold back the adoption of new technologies. Their purpose is to identify the conditions required for a solution to fulfil the function for which it was selected.

From promise to operation

Analysing a technology comprehensively means recognising that its performance does not depend on equipment alone. Infrastructure, energy, materials, supply chains, information, maintenance and human and institutional capabilities also shape the outcome.

Describing a technology is therefore not the same as recommending it. Its suitability must be assessed against a baseline, other available alternatives and the particular conditions of the place where it is intended to operate.

The energy transition moves forward when solutions can be put into operation, sustained over time and shown to be fulfilling their purpose. Announcements and targets can set the direction; turning them into results requires prepared systems.
